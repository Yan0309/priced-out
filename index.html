Half of renters cross the 30% line.

The national picture

The 30% rule is a warning light, not a finish line.

Housing researchers commonly describe households that spend more than 30% of income on rent as cost-burdened. Above 50%, the burden is considered severe.

The rule is not a physical threshold. It is a useful benchmark that leaves less room for food, health care, transportation, and savings. In the latest five-year ACS estimates, roughly half of all U.S. renter households sit above it.

01 / The threshold National estimate

01

A simple benchmark exposes a large problem.

A household is cost-burdened when gross rent takes 30% or more of income. In the U.S., 51.1% of renter households cross that line after excluding units where the ratio could not be computed.

Gross rent includes utilities paid by the renter.

02

At the median, rent already consumes nearly one-third of income.

Median gross rent is $1,413 per month. Median renter household income is $52,966 per year, or about $4,414 per month. Rent alone equals 32% of that income before other necessities enter the budget.

A median describes the middle household, not the typical budget of every renter.

03

The burden is geographic, not random.

County estimates reveal clusters of high rent burden in Florida, coastal metros, mountain resort communities, and university centers. Large stretches of the Midwest and Appalachia sit lower.

County estimates combine five years of survey responses.

04

Florida turns the map red.

Seven of the ten highest-burden large counties are in Florida. Osceola County leads the group: 65.4% of renter households are cost-burdened, and 35.2% are severely burdened.

Large counties here means at least 10,000 renter households.

05

The national average hides a wide spread.

State estimates range from about 39% to just over 60%. The national rate is useful for a headline, but it smooths over housing markets with very different rules, prices, and incomes.

Each dot is a state or the District of Columbia.

06

Higher county income does not reliably mean lower rent burden.

Across 2,355 counties with at least 1,000 renter households, the fitted line is almost flat. Median renter income explains less than 1% of the variation in county rent-burden rates.

This is an ecological comparison: counties are the observations, not individual households.

07

Two counties, similar incomes, different rent math.

Osceola County, Florida and Ward County, North Dakota have almost the same median renter income: about $53,400. Yet their cost-burdened shares differ by 27 percentage points. Their median rents differ by nearly $700 per month.

A comparison can reveal a pattern without proving what caused it.

08

Rent and income together tell a stronger statistical story.

In a county-level model with both variables, each additional $10,000 in median renter income is associated with an 8.3-point lower burden rate, holding median rent constant. Each $100 in rent is associated with a 3.6-point higher rate, holding income constant. Together they explain about 66% of the variation.

These are adjusted associations, not causal effects.

What the data can say

Rent burden is a ratio, and every ratio has two sides.

01

Income matters.

Higher-income places tend to have lower burden rates when rent is held constant. But income alone leaves most county variation unexplained.

02

Housing costs matter too.

The combined income-and-rent model fits much better. That does not mean either variable has a simple causal effect.

03

The unit of analysis matters.

County-level patterns cannot be applied directly to individual households. Aggregation can hide unequal burdens within a place.

Methods & sources

Reproducible by design.

Estimates come from the U.S. Census Bureau’s American Community Survey 2020–2024 five-year release, accessed through the Census Reporter API. The five-year estimates describe conditions over 2020–2024, not a single calendar year.

Definitions

Cost-burdened renter households have gross rent equal to 30% or more of household income in the past 12 months. Severely burdened households have gross rent equal to 50% or more. Households with “not computed” rent-to-income ratios are excluded from the denominator.

Statistical notes

Charts show point estimates from sample survey data. Margins of error are retained in the downloadable CSV files. The county regressions use ordinary least squares and include counties with at least 1,000 renter households and complete estimates. The two-variable model uses median renter household income and median gross rent as predictors.

Limitations

County summaries are not household-level observations. Cost-burden rates also depend on household composition, housing supply, local policy, migration, and measurement choices. The models describe associations and should not be interpreted as causal estimates.
